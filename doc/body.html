A portfolio optimiser that runs inside the same q/kdb+ stack as the market data and the fills, and re-solves as each fill arrives. The classic mean–variance problem — maximise expected return, minus factor risk, specific risk and transaction costs, subject to the holdings adding up to 100% of the capital and a maximum holding in each stock — is turned into OSQP's input matrices directly by our own q code (.po.blocks), with no modelling tool such as CVXPY in between. OSQP (Operator Splitting Quadratic Program from University of Oxford) is called from q through a 180-line C file (a “shim”), which q loads with 2: once it is compiled with clang against KX's k.h header. OSQP stays loaded between solves and remembers its last answer. When a fill arrives only two vectors change, the bounds l and u, so it re-solves starting from that answer (a “warm” re-solve): 9 ms at 2,000 stocks and 24 ms at 5,000, against 0.3 and 2.1 seconds to solve from scratch (a “cold” start). The same builder stacks periods into a multi-period problem and strategies into one net book, and the whole thing subscribes to the chained tickerplant of Anatomy of a Fill, so the fills that TCA scores are the fills that move the book.

Why Re-optimise on a Fill

A companion to the trilogy. Part One, The Order & Trade Lifecycle, is the map; Part Two, Anatomy of a Fill, the territory; Part Three, Sharded Ticks, the machine. This one is the decision that comes before the order: what the book should hold, and how that answer changes as the order fills.

Companion. Anatomy of a Fill scores each fill against a benchmark. This document takes the same fills as inputs: each one moves the book, and the book is what the optimiser optimises.The whole argument in one paragraph. A portfolio optimiser answers one question: given what I expect, what I fear and what it costs to move, what should I hold? The textbook asks it once a day. But the inputs move all day, and the biggest one, the book you hold now, moves on every fill. OSQP is a solver built to be asked the same question over and over with a few numbers changed: it keeps its factorisation, starts from its last answer, and takes a few dozen iterations instead of a few thousand. Put it in a q process behind the tickerplant, write the problem in its one shape, and re-optimising on a fill costs milliseconds. The rest of the document is that idea, worked through, with the numbers measured.

Three Ways to Run It

Single. One book, one period: the target portfolio for now, re-optimised on every batch of fills. The default, and the case the labs measure first.

MPO, multi-period: one book, several sessions. A $2bn long-only fund decides, after an earnings revision, to rotate out of financials into technology. Doing it all today would cost too much in market impact. MPO plans the rotation over three sessions — say 40% of the financials sold today, 35% tomorrow and the balance on day three — and executes only today's slice, so it does not chase an alpha signal that is already decaying. The next morning it is re-optimised from the holdings actually left and refreshed forecasts. That plan–execute–re-plan loop is model predictive control (MPC), the engineering name for it: a satnav does the same, planning the whole route, taking the first turn, then re-planning from where the car actually is. MPO run this way is MPC applied to a portfolio. Typical users: large or capacity-constrained books, liquidations, portfolio transitions and index rebalances.

MSO, multi-strategy: several books, one execution desk. On a multi-manager platform at 10:00, the momentum pod wants to buy 500k NVDA and the reversal pod wants to sell 400k. MSO optimises the books jointly and sends the desk only the net 100k buy; the other 400k crosses internally, with no spread or market impact paid. Each PM keeps their own book, risk limits and P&L attribution. Typical users: multi-manager (pod) hedge funds and bank desks internalising flow.

In short: MPO decides when to trade; MSO decides whose trade it is. Both are set with one flag; §4 has the mathematics, the diagrams and when each runs.

1The Classic Problem

Markowitz as a desk writes it, one piece at a time: what the book is expected to earn, what it risks, what it costs to get there, and the rules it must keep. Each piece below becomes a block of rows in the matrix OSQP solves.

1.1Maximise Expected Return

Factor model. \(V = X F X^{\top} + \Delta\): X is the n × k exposure of each asset to each factor, F the k × k factor covariance, Δ the diagonal of specific variances. These are Barra's letters, used throughout. With k = 50 it replaces n² numbers with about 50n; 1.2a draws it.Error maximisation. α is a forecast, and the optimiser treats it as fact: it buys most of the names whose α was overestimated most. Michaud called this error maximisation. Shrinking α toward zero, or toward what the factor model implies, is the usual guard.n assets \(a_1,\dots,a_n\); \(\alpha\), their expected returns over the horizon; \(h\), the weights, each a fraction of NAV. Alone, the objective is linear, and its optimum is to put everything in the asset with the highest α up to its limit: a concentrated bet, which is why nobody stops here.

1.2Minimise Risk and Transaction Costs

Choosing λ. λ is the price of variance in units of expected return. Desks rarely pick it directly: they set it so the solution lands on a target ex-ante volatility or tracking error. When costs and constraints don't bind, doubling λ halves the active bets.Match the horizons. α is earned over a horizon; a cost is paid once, when you trade. A one-day α against a round-trip cost of 10 bps will not trade much. Scale α to the holding period, or the costs to a per-period amortisation.Risk is the variance of the book's return, \(h^{\top}V h\), weighed by a risk aversion \(\lambda > 0\). The specific risk \(\Delta \) is the diagonal of \(V\), not a separate term beside it. Costs are a function of the trade \(h - h_0\), from the book held now, never of the holding: a position that stays where it is costs nothing to keep. In shares, a trade in asset \(a_i\) is \((h_i - h_{0,i})\,p/\psi_i\) for a book worth \(p\) and a price \(\psi_i\).

1.2aEquity Multiple-Factor Models

Barra. Barr Rosenberg built the first commercial models of this kind in the mid-1970s; Barra is now part of MSCI. This document uses the Barra Risk Model Handbook's symbols throughout, and OSQP's only for the solver; the table below is the key. Equity only.11Multiple-factor model (MFM). A model in which several common influences move many stocks at once, each stock in proportion to how sensitive it is to each one, plus one event of its own per stock that no factor explains.Most of what moves a stock is shared with other stocks: the market rises, banks fall together, small companies have a bad month. A multiple-factor model names those shared influences, the factors, and measures how much each stock responds to each one. Whatever the factors cannot explain is the stock's own, its specific return. Barra's equity models are the best-known example, and the risk numbers most desks see each morning come from one of them or a close relative.

aFactor Return Calculation

Estimation universe. The set of representative stocks in a market that the regression is run on each month, for example the larger, regularly traded names. Exposures are standardised against it too.Each month, every stock's return is written as its exposures times the factor returns, plus its own part. Stacked for all \(n\) stocks it is one matrix equation, with one row per stock:

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} r_{1} \\ r_{2} \\ \vdots \\ r_{n} \end{bmatrix}}_{r} \;=\; \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} x_{1,1} & x_{1,2} & \cdots & x_{1,k} \\ x_{2,1} & x_{2,2} & \cdots & x_{2,k} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ x_{n,1} & x_{n,2} & \cdots & x_{n,k} \end{bmatrix}}_{X} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} f_{1} \\ f_{2} \\ \vdots \\ f_{k} \end{bmatrix}}_{f} \;+\; \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} u_{1} \\ u_{2} \\ \vdots \\ u_{n} \end{bmatrix}}_{u} \]

Row \(i\) reads: stock \(i\)'s return is \(x_{i,1}f_1 + x_{i,2}f_2 + \dots + x_{i,k}f_k + u_i\). The returns \(r\) are known at the end of the month and the exposures \(X\) at its start. The factor returns \(f\) are the unknowns: the \(k\) numbers that best explain all \(n\) returns at once, found by a cross-sectional regression — one regression across the stocks, not through time — weighted so that larger companies count for more (the square root of market cap is a common choice). What the regression cannot explain is \(u\). Doing this every month builds a history of \(f\) and of \(u\), which is what the next three steps use.

bExposures: Risk Indices and Industries

Risk-index exposure. A stock's score on one style factor, standardised so that the cap-weighted average stock scores 0 and most stocks fall between about −5 and +5. A higher score means the stock is more exposed to that factor.Indicator. One raw measurement, such as earnings ÷ price or last year's return; Barra calls these descriptors. Some use only market data (last year's return), some only company accounts (debt ÷ assets), and some combine the two: earnings ÷ price sets the market's valuation against what the firm earns. A risk index blends several: a volatility index might combine short-term volatility, long-term volatility and how much the stock moves with the market.Barra's factors come in two kinds. Risk indices, also called styles, are continuous scores built from company data: Size, Value, Momentum, Volatility, Growth, Leverage, Liquidity and so on. Each starts as one or more indicators, raw measurements that are standardised against the estimation universe:

\[ \text{standardised indicator} \;=\; \frac{\text{raw value} \;-\; \text{mean}}{\text{standard deviation}} \]

Momentum, in everyday use, is the rate at which a price, an economy or a trend is speeding up. As a Barra risk index, Price Momentum scores each stock on its recent relative performance: last year's winners score high. It is one of the four styles Barra treats as global.so that +1 always means one standard deviation above the average stock, whatever the units of the raw number. Industries are the other kind: a stock belongs to one (exposure 1, the rest 0), or, for a conglomerate, is split by its business — Barra's example is Walt Disney, 65% media and 35% entertainment. A typical single-country model has a few dozen industries and around a dozen risk indices; the U.S. model the handbook describes has 68 factors in all.

cCovariance Matrix Calculation

Not our λ. In this subsection λ is Barra's decay factor for old data, between 0 and 1. Everywhere else in this document λ is the risk aversion of 1.2. Likewise κ in (d) below is Barra's scaling constant, not the cost κ.The history of factor returns gives the factor covariance \(F\): the variance of each factor down the diagonal, and how each pair moves together off it. The simplest estimate counts every past month equally. Markets change, so Barra gives recent months more weight with an exponential decay set by a half-life:

\[ \lambda \;=\; 0.5^{\,1/\text{HALF-LIFE}} \qquad\qquad w(t) \;=\; \lambda^{\,T-t} \]

Half-life. The age at which an observation counts half as much as this month's. With 48 months, a month four years ago counts half, eight years ago a quarter. Longer is steadier; shorter reacts faster but throws more data away.\(T\) is this month and \(t\) any month in the past, so an observation one half-life old gets half the weight of the newest. Barra's global equity model uses a half-life of 48 months. Many models also scale \(F\) so that the risk it predicts for a market index, such as the S&P 500 or Japan's TSE1, matches a separate short-term forecast of that index's volatility (a GARCH model, or an exponentially weighted daily volatility), because calm and turbulent periods tend to cluster. The result, in Barra's layout, is a \(k \times k\) matrix:

\[ F \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} \operatorname{Var}(f_1) & \operatorname{Cov}(f_1, f_2) & \cdots & \operatorname{Cov}(f_1, f_k) \\ \operatorname{Cov}(f_2, f_1) & \operatorname{Var}(f_2) & \cdots & \operatorname{Cov}(f_2, f_k) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \operatorname{Cov}(f_k, f_1) & \operatorname{Cov}(f_k, f_2) & \cdots & \operatorname{Var}(f_k) \end{bmatrix} \]

The saving is the point. For 1,400 stocks a full covariance has 980,700 distinct numbers to estimate; with 68 factors, \(F\) has 2,346. Fewer numbers means less chance of finding relationships that are only noise.

dSpecific Risk Modelling

Is it a linear regression? Yes, two of them. The average level is a time-series regression of this month's average on past months'. The relative level is a cross-sectional regression of each stock's (relative) size of surprise on its characteristics. They model the size of the specific return, \(\lvert u\rvert\), rather than its square, because absolute values are less thrown by one wild month.Each stock's specific risk is the standard deviation of its specific return \(u_i\). Rather than take the raw history, Barra forecasts it as the product of how noisy stocks are on average this month and how noisy this stock is relative to the average:

\[ \hat\sigma_{it} \;=\; \kappa_{it}\,\big(1 + \hat V_{it}\big)\,\hat S_t \qquad \hat S_t \;=\; \alpha + \sum_{i=1}^{k} \beta_i\, S_{t-i} \qquad \hat V_{it} \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{K} Z_{ikt}\,\gamma_k \]

\(\hat S_t\) is the forecast average size of specific returns across the universe this month, predicted from the realised averages \(S_{t-1}, S_{t-2}, \dots\) of recent months. \(\hat V_{it}\) is stock \(i\)'s forecast size relative to that average, predicted from its characteristics \(Z_{ikt}\) (its industry, its size, its volatility and so on), each weighted by \(\gamma_k\). \(\kappa\) converts an average absolute surprise into a standard deviation. Squared and laid on a diagonal, these forecasts are \(\Delta\).

eFrom the Model to the Covariance

Substituting \(r = Xf + u\) into the variance of returns, and using that \(u\) is uncorrelated with the factors and across stocks:

\[ \text{Risk} = \operatorname{Var}(r) = \operatorname{Var}(Xf + u) = \operatorname{Var}(Xf) + \operatorname{Var}(u) = X F X^{\top} + \Delta \]
e·2A Portfolio of Many Stocks

A portfolio holds many stocks, each with a weight \(h_{p,i}\). Its return is the weighted sum of theirs, and because each stock's return is factors plus its own part, so is the portfolio's — Barra's EQ 1-4:

\[ r_p \;=\; \sum_{k} x_{p,k}\, f_k \;+\; \sum_{i} h_{p,i}\, u_i \qquad\qquad x_{p,k} \;=\; \sum_{i} h_{p,i}\, x_{i,k} \]

The portfolio has exposures of its own, \(x_{p,k}\): the holdings-weighted average of its stocks' exposures. A book 60% in a stock with Size +2 and 40% in one with Size −1 has Size +0.8. That vector is the \(x_p = X^{\top}h\) the optimiser carries as variables (1.3).

fFinal Risk Calculation

A portfolio's risk combines its weights with the covariance — Barra's EQ 1-11:

\[ \sigma_p \;=\; \sqrt{\,h_p^{\top}\,\big(X F X^{\top} + \Delta\big)\,h_p\,} \qquad h_p = \begin{bmatrix} h_1 \\ \vdots \\ h_n \end{bmatrix} \]

In practice it is computed in two pieces that never form the \(n \times n\) matrix: the portfolio's factor exposures \(x_p = X^{\top}h_p\), \(k\) numbers, give the factor risk \(x_p^{\top}Fx_p\); the specific risk is \(\sum_i h_i^2\,\Delta_{ii}\). That is exactly the form the optimiser uses, with \(x_p = X^{\top}h\) (1.7).

gBenchmarks, Active Risk and Residual Return

Residual return is the part of a stock's or portfolio's return that has nothing to do with the market: total return minus beta times the market's return. Over many stocks it averages out, so it is also called diversifiable return.Beta (β) measures how much a stock moves with the market. β = 1.5: when the market is 10% above cash, the stock is expected to be 15% above it. Barra's predicted beta comes from the factor model rather than from a regression on old prices, so it updates as soon as the company changes.A benchmark is the reference portfolio a fund is judged against — usually a published index of stocks weighted by market value. Common equity examples are the S&P 500 (large U.S. companies), the Russell 1000 Growth (U.S. large-cap growth stocks), the FTSE 100 (the UK's largest), TOPIX (Japan) and MSCI World (large companies across developed markets). An active manager tries to beat it, so what matters is the difference: the active weights \(h_{PA} = h_p - h_B\), the portfolio's weight in each stock minus the benchmark's.

\[ \text{active risk} \;=\; \sqrt{\,h_{PA}^{\top}\big(XFX^{\top} + \Delta\big)\,h_{PA}\,} \qquad h_{PA} = h_p - h_B \]

Active risk, also called tracking error, is the standard deviation of the active return: how far the fund's return is likely to wander from the benchmark's. It uses the same model; only the weights change. A fund that holds the benchmark exactly has no active risk at all, however risky the market is. In this document's problem, writing the risk term on \(h - h_B\) instead of \(h\) turns the optimiser into one that trades alpha against tracking error.

g·2Historical Beta vs. Predicted Beta

Why it matters here. A beta-neutral constraint, \(x_{p,\text{mkt}} = 0\), is only as good as the betas in \(X\). With predicted betas the hedge moves the day the company changes; with historical ones it lags.Historical beta is measured after the fact: regress a stock's excess returns on the market's, usually over the last 60 months. It has two weaknesses. It is slow to notice a company that has changed — after RJR Nabisco spun off its tobacco business in 1999 its risk changed sharply, but a 60-month beta caught up only slowly. And it is bent by one-off events: Union Carbide's 1984 Bhopal disaster happened in a rising market, so its historical beta came out artificially low. Predicted beta, also called fundamental beta, is computed from the factor model instead: from the stock's current exposures to size, volatility, yield and its industry, and the factor covariance,

\[ \beta_i \;=\; \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(r_i, r_B)}{\operatorname{Var}(r_B)} \;=\; \frac{\big(V h_B\big)_i}{h_B^{\top} V h_B} \]

Because the exposures are refreshed every month, a predicted beta follows the business as it changes, and it does not carry the memory of an accident. Barra's applications use it for that reason, and it is what the market column of \(X\) in .po.mfm stands in for.

hGlobal Equity Factors

Barra's single-country models are linked into one global model by a small set of global equity factors: a world factor, the global market's return; one country factor per country; global industry factors; and four global risk indices — Price Momentum, Size, Value and Volatility. Each country's industry factor responds one-for-one to the world factor, its country and its global industry; each local style that matches one of the four responds one-for-one to it. Their returns come from a constrained, weighted cross-sectional regression of the local factor returns each month, and their covariance uses the 48-month half-life above. The four styles are the ones that behave alike across markets; the rest stay local, because the same name, say Growth, can behave quite differently in Tokyo and New York.

  • Exposures as variables (1.3, 1.7): the book's exposure \(x_p = X^{\top}h\) is \(k\) numbers. Carrying it as variables keeps the \(n \times n\) matrix from ever being formed.
  • Constraints: bounds on \(x_p\) are factor limits. \(x_{p,\text{mkt}} = 0\) is beta-neutral; \(\lvert x_{p,\text{mom}}\rvert \le 0.1\) caps the momentum bet at a tenth of a standard deviation.
  • Alpha: many signals are factor tilts in disguise. A momentum signal and a momentum exposure are the same column, so the optimiser will hedge away a signal that is all factor unless its bounds allow the bet.
  • Attribution: a book's return splits the same way, \(x_p^{\top}f\) from the factors and \(h_p^{\top}u\) from stock picking, which is how a PM's risk report reads.
  • Estimation (1.6): with 2,000 names and 500 days of history the sample covariance is singular; the factor model is the usual cure, because \(\Delta\) is positive and \(F\) is small enough to estimate well.

1.5Transaction Costs

A real cost has two parts. A linear part, \(\kappa_i\,\lvert h_i - h_{0,i}\rvert\), for the half-spread and fees, which can differ for buys and sells. And a part that grows faster than the trade, for market impact: Almgren and Chriss's linear temporary impact makes it quadratic, \(\eta_i (h_i - h_{0,i})^2\), which is what keeps the problem a QP.4 The absolute value is not quadratic either, so the trade is split into buys and sells, \(h - h_0 = b - s\) with \(b, s \ge 0\); with \(\kappa > 0\) the optimum never buys and sells one name, so \(\kappa^{\top}(b+s) = \kappa^{\top}\lvert h - h_0\rvert\). Separate buy and sell rates are then just different coefficients on b and s.10

1.6Sample Covariance Matrix from Stock Returns

Let \(R\) be the \(n \times t\) matrix of \(t\) observed returns on \(n\) stocks. Assuming the returns are independent and identically distributed through time, the sample covariance is

\[ V \;=\; \frac{1}{t}\, R\left(I - \tfrac{1}{t}\mathbf 1 \mathbf 1^{\top}\right) R^{\top} \;=\; G\,G^{\top}, \qquad G = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt t}\, R\left(I - \tfrac{1}{t}\mathbf 1 \mathbf 1^{\top}\right) \]

Its rank is at most \(t-1\). With two years of daily returns, \(t \approx 500\), so for 2,000 names it is singular: some portfolios look riskless that are not, and the optimiser finds them. That is the practical case for a factor model (1.2a), or for shrinking the sample estimate toward one.

1.7Don't Form the Covariance Matrix

Fernando's advice — pass a factor of the covariance to the optimiser, not the matrix itself — holds, and for three reasons.10 Size: \(V\) is \(n^2\) dense numbers, 4 million at 2,000 names, while a factor is \(n \times t\) or \(n \times k\). Accuracy: forming \(RR^{\top}\) squares the condition number, and a singular \(V\) (1.6) is where rounding turns a zero eigenvalue negative. Speed: the solver's linear algebra stays sparse.

One refinement: a Cholesky factorisation of \(V\) still forms \(V\) first, costs \(n^3/3\), and fails on the singular matrix of 1.6 without pivoting. The data already hold a factor: \(G\) from 1.6 gives \(h^{\top}V h = \lVert G^{\top}h\rVert^2\) with no factorisation at all. In OSQP that is new variables \(x_p = G^{\top}h\) with \(2\lambda I\) on them in P — exactly how this stack carries the factor model, with \(X\) and \(F\) in place of \(G\). Cholesky is the right tool only when all you are given is \(V\).

3Incremental, Intraday

Cvxportfolio is iterative, but per rebalancing period rather than per fill: its multi-period policy plans H trades, executes the first and solves again next period, through CVXPY on every call. It is a research and backtesting library, GPLv3, and the right cross-check for this stack rather than its engine (C3). cvxopt adds nothing here: OSQP and Clarabel cover its problem types.Yes, it exists, and it is the same problem asked again with one input changed. A rebalance sends a list of target trades to the execution desk; while those orders work, every fill changes the book \(h_0\). Re-optimise from scratch and you pay a cold solve each time. Re-optimise incrementally and you pay for the change: the constraint matrix is the same, so its factorisation is kept, and the last answer is a very good first guess for the next one.

Three things make this more than a speed trick. The target stays honest: an order that has half-filled at a bad price changes what the rest of the book should be. The turnover and participation budgets stay honest: the fills have spent part of them, and the problem knows it. And the same loop is where an alpha update, a new risk number or a limit change lands mid-day, as one more vector or matrix update to a problem that is already set up (B2).

4Three Modes: Single, Multi-Period, Multi-Strategy

Iterative is not multi-period. Iterative is how often you solve: again on every batch of fills, milliseconds apart. Multi-period is how far one solve looks ahead: several days. Any mode can be re-solved iteratively; only MPO looks ahead.Three clocks. Lookback: the past n days of prices, used to estimate X, F, Δ and α. Period: how far ahead MPO plans, usually days. Fill: when h₀ moves, milliseconds. Only the fill clock runs intraday.The optimiser runs in one of three modes, set by one flag: q opt.q :5013 -p 5015 -mode single, mpo or mso. They are not three solvers. All three are the same block (Fig. 11), placed once, H times or with S books. What differs is the question each answers, and the clock it runs on.

Single is the problem of 1.3: one book, one period. It answers what should the book be now? It knows only the book held now, \(h_0\), and today's forecasts:

\[ \max_{h}\;\; \alpha^{\top}h \;-\; \lambda\, h^{\top}V h \;-\; \phi(h - h_0) \qquad h \in \mathcal W \]

where \(\phi\) is the cost of the trade, \(\kappa^{\top}\lvert\cdot\rvert + \eta^{\top}(\cdot)^2\), and \(\mathcal W\) is the budget, box, factor and turnover limits. It runs on every batch of fills, warm, in about a millisecond.

MPC is model predictive control, from control engineering: plan the next H moves with a model of the future, make the first, measure where you actually are, plan again. Also called a receding horizon. MPO is portfolio optimisation run that way.1 Are they the same? In practice, yes, and the names are used interchangeably. Strictly, MPO is the problem (optimise H periods at once) and MPC is the way it is used (trade the first period, re-solve). Solve MPO once and trade the whole plan, and it is MPO without MPC — mistake 10.Where the future comes from. Nothing in MPO is future data. \(\alpha_t = \delta^{t-1}\alpha\) is today's forecast, faded; V and the costs are today's estimates held fixed. The future books \(h_2,\dots,h_H\) are unknowns the solver chooses, not inputs.MPO, multi-period, keeps one book but looks H periods ahead, usually days. It answers what should the book be today, given what I will want tomorrow and the day after? Each period has its own book \(h_t\) and its own forecast \(\alpha_t\), and each period's trade starts from the book the previous period left:

\[ \max_{h_1,\dots,h_H}\;\; \sum_{t=1}^{H} \Big[\, \alpha_t^{\top}h_t \;-\; \lambda\, h_t^{\top}V h_t \;-\; \phi(h_t - h_{t-1}) \,\Big] \qquad h_t \in \mathcal W,\;\; \alpha_t = \delta^{t-1}\alpha \]

Only \(h_1\) is traded. \(h_2,\dots,h_H\) are there to stop \(h_1\) from over-trading a signal that will be half gone in a few days, and are thrown away: tomorrow the problem is solved again from the book actually held. It runs cold once a period, overnight, in seconds. Its day-1 result sets today's target and turnover for the intraday loop, and it can also be re-solved warm on fills, in tens of milliseconds.

A blend is not multi-strategy. 0.1 value + 0.8 momentum + 0.1 profitability is one strategy: one α, \(\alpha = 0.1\,\alpha_{\text{val}} + 0.8\,\alpha_{\text{mom}} + 0.1\,\alpha_{\text{prof}}\), one book, one set of limits. That is Single. MSO is for strategies that are run apart — their own capital, PM, limits and P&L — and meet only at the execution desk.The four books in the labs and in -mode mso are illustrative: momentum, value, short-term reversal and event (earnings, index changes), each a random α with an equal share of capital. A real platform would plug its own signals in.MSO, multi-strategy, keeps one period but splits the book among S strategies that share one execution desk. A book is the portfolio one strategy holds: its weights \(h_s\), its capital \(c_s\), its own limits and its own P&L. So "four strategies" here means four books, each run separately, whose trades go out through one desk. It answers what should each strategy hold, if their trades are netted before they go out? Here the subscript is a strategy, not a period:

\[ \max_{h_1,\dots,h_S}\;\; \sum_{s=1}^{S} c_s\,\alpha_s^{\top}h_s \;-\; \lambda\, h^{\top}V h \;-\; \phi(h - h_0) \qquad h = \sum_{s} c_s h_s,\;\; h_s \in \mathcal W_s \]

Each strategy earns its own alpha on its share of capital \(c_s\) and keeps its own budget and box; the firm carries the risk on the net book \(h\) and pays costs on the net trade only, so two books trading the same name in opposite directions cross for free (C2). It runs on the same clock as Single, on every batch of fills.

One Unit Convention

Weights are fractions of NAV: 0.01 is one percent of the book. α is expected return per year and V is variance per year, so \(\alpha^{\top}h\) and \(\lambda h^{\top}V h\) are in return per year. Costs \(\kappa\) and \(\eta\) are per unit of weight traded: kappa = 5e-4 is 5 bps of the notional traded. A shares trade becomes a weight change as qty * px % NAV, signed +1 for a buy. Every bps figure in this document is 1e4 * one of these.

OSQP minimises, so the objective it sees is the negative of the utility above: −αᵀh + risk + cost. A lower OSQP objective is a better portfolio.

OSQP Has One Shape

OSQP solves exactly one problem: \(\min_x \tfrac12 x^{\top}Px + q^{\top}x\) subject to \(l \le Ax \le u\), with P positive semidefinite. There is no modelling language in front of it. An equality is a row with \(l = u\); a one-sided inequality has \(l = -\infty\) or \(u = +\infty\) (here, anything beyond 1e30); a bound on one variable is a row of A with a single 1 in it. Everything the portfolio needs is written in those terms by port.q, and that rewriting is the modelling: q is the modelling language, by hand.

Two rewrites carry the whole problem. The absolute value in the linear cost becomes two non-negative variables, buys b and sells s, with \(h - b + s = h_0\); because κ is positive, the optimum never buys and sells the same name, so \(\kappa(b+s) = \kappa|h-h_0|\). The dense covariance becomes k extra variables \(x_p = X^{\top}h\), so \(h^{\top}X F X^{\top}h = x_p^{\top}F x_p\) and P stays diagonal plus one k × k block.

One Handle per Problem

The shim returns a long, not a solver: hs:.po.setup[b;s]. The solver itself, its factorisation, its scaling and its last solution stay in C, in one of 256 slots, until .qp.free hs. That is what makes the fill loop cheap — the q side sends a few vectors and gets one back — and it is what lets one q process keep many problems warm at once: one per strategy, one per horizon, one for the firm. A slot is about 5.6 MB per thousand names (Fig. 21).

The Source

About 450 lines: a C shim of 180 and four q files of about 270, plus the tests and benchmarks that check them. It lives at github.com/rohanshiloh/port-opt-stack. The Problem

Every problem in this document is built from the same piece of code, .po.blocks. It writes one period of the problem — the weights, the factor rows, the trade split, the budget, the box and the turnover — as matrix entries, at whatever row and column offset it is given. The three problem types differ only in where they place that block in the big matrix:

  • Single period (.po.single): the block once, at the top left. The block is the problem; Fig. 10 shows what is inside it.
  • Multi-period (.po.mpo): one copy per period down the diagonal, each with that period's decayed alpha, plus a −I on each later period's trade rows, under the previous period's weights, so each period's trade starts from the book the one before it left.
  • Multi-strategy (.po.mso): the block once for the firm's net book, with a group of columns added to its left for each strategy's weights, that strategy's own budget and box rows beneath them, and net rows that tie the firm's weights to the capital-weighted sum of the strategies'.

So there is one formulation to get right, and the other two reuse it: a bug in the factor rows or the trade split shows up in all three, and so does a fix.

The rest of this section is that block: its notation, each of its terms as a formula beside the q that writes it, and the table a reader keeps open while debugging.

A1Triplets to CSC

Run in OPT

Every block of the problem is written as triplets — (row; column; value) — because that is how a person thinks about a matrix: "row rT+i, column i, a one." OSQP wants compressed sparse column: per column, its row indices in order and its values, and a vector of where each column starts. .qp.csc does the conversion with one sort and one amend: sort by i+m*j, count the entries in each column with @[n#0;j;+;1], and sum the counts.

P must be the upper triangle only. OSQP mirrors it; pass the whole matrix and the off-diagonal terms are counted twice, silently.

/ qp.q — one sort key, one amend
.qp.csc:{[m;n;i;j;v]
  m:`long$m; n:`long$n; i:`long$i; j:`long$j;
  o:iasc i+m*j;
  if[count[o]>count distinct i+m*j; '"csc: duplicate (row;col) entry"];
  i:i o; j:j o;
  (m; n; 0,sums @[n#0;j;+;1]; i; `float$v o) };

B1A Fill Is Two Vectors

Run in OPT

A fill of q shares at p moves one name's weight by q*p%NAV. In the problem, that is the bounds of one trade row — h0 sits in both l and u there — and the upper bound of the turnover row, which falls by what the fill used. Nothing in P or A moves, so OSQP keeps its factorisation, and the last solution is the start of the next.

Measured through the shim at 2,000 names: the first solve took 1,025 iterations and 305 ms; forty fills later, the median re-solve was 8.6 ms, at 28 iterations on average. Solving each of those problems from scratch took 410 ms.

Spend the turnover. A loop that moves h0 but leaves τ alone lets the optimiser trade its whole budget again after every fill, and the day trades many times what the rebalance allowed.

C1Multi-period (MPO)

Run in OPT

Stack H copies of the period block down the diagonal. Period t gets alpha α·δᵗ, and its trade rows carry an extra −1 under the previous period's weights, so its trade is measured from where period t−1 left the book; only the first period's trade rows carry h0. Solve, execute the first period, and plan again next period with the new book.

In the Python mirror at 800 names with δ = 0.7 and a 10% turnover per period, every period used its full budget and the book's expected alpha rose for three periods and then eased (0.44%, 0.63%, 0.70%, 0.67%). With H = 1 the stacked builder gives the single-period objective to within two parts in a million, and with one strategy the multi-strategy builder does the same: the test that the coupling is right.

It is H times the problem. At 2,000 names a cold solve went from 0.3 s (H = 1) to 3.7 s (H = 2) and 45 s (H = 4) — Clarabel took 1.0 and 9.7 s — but a warm re-solve after a fill stayed at 36 and 76 ms. Solve the horizon cold once a period, overnight when a period is a day; re-solve it warm on fills if the plan should track the day, or hand its first period to the intraday loop (Fig. 8).

C2Multi-strategy (MSO)

Run in OPT

S strategies, each with its own alpha, share of capital c_s, budget and box. Two ways to run them. Optimise, then net: each solves its own problem against its own book, paying its own costs, and the firm sums the trades. Net, then optimise: one problem whose variables are every strategy's weights plus the net book, with the firm's risk and costs charged on the net trade only.

Four strategies on 1,000 names (synthetic alphas standing in for momentum, value, reversal and event books), scored the same way on the firm (alpha on each book, risk on the net book, costs on the net trade): net-then-optimise raised firm utility from 133 to 153 bps when the alphas were independent, 131 to 154 at an overlap of 0.4, and 132 to 166 at 0.8, for at most the same 10% of net turnover. The strategies' own books traded 41–60% between them; 76–83% of that crossed internally.

The joint problem moves each book far more than that book would move alone, because a cross costs nothing. That is the gain, and it is also a governance question: each strategy's P&L now depends on the others, and the firm needs a transfer price for internal crosses before it runs this live.

C3Against Cvxportfolio

Run in shell

Cvxportfolio is the library behind the multi-period paper this stack's MPO follows.1 Given the same problem, its first-period trade should be ours. bench/cvxp_check.py writes the problem once in each: our triplets solved by OSQP at a tight tolerance, and Cvxportfolio 1.5.0 with every input supplied by hand (market_data=None), so nothing is estimated from history.

At 300 names and 10 factors the two agreed for H = 1, 2 and 4: the first-period trades differed by at most 0.0016 of an average weight, both traded the same 18 names, and both spent exactly the 10% turnover budget.

Speed is a different matter. Each execute rebuilds the problem through CVXPY: 0.5–0.65 s a call at 300 names and about 3.3 s at 1,000, with no saving on the second call; at 2,000 names its first call did not finish in ten minutes, and CVXPY warned that the problem has too many parameters for efficient compilation. Two mappings to get right. Cvxportfolio's turnover is half the l1 norm of the trade, so our τ is its TurnoverLimit(tau/2). Its impact term is a 3/2 power scaled by volatility and volume, not our quadratic, so the check sets η = 0 on both sides. And install it in its own virtual environment: pip resolved it here against an older OSQP Python package than the rest of bench/ uses.

D1Cold, Warm and ρ

Run in OPTshell

The experiment behind every timing in this document: build the problem, solve it cold, then forty fills, each moving 1% of names a quarter of the way to target and spending the turnover it used, each followed by a warm re-solve; then the first three of those problems solved again from scratch.

The first run used OSQP's default settings and was a surprise: 21 seconds and 24,925 iterations for a cold solve at 5,000 names, where Clarabel took 0.67 s. OSQP's adaptive ρ was estimating about 1.2 and updating once; this problem wants something nearer 100. Setting ρ by hand to n/70 cut the cold solve seven to ten times — 2.8 s to 0.31 s at 2,000 names, 21 s to 2.1 s at 5,000 — at the same accuracy (D2). The rule is fitted to this formulation and these units; refit it if you change either.

With ρ tuned, OSQP and Clarabel are level from a cold start at 1,000 names (72 and 69 ms), and Clarabel is 1.6 to 4.7 times faster above that. After a fill, OSQP's median re-solve is 3.5, 8.6, 24 and 59 ms at 1, 2, 5 and 10 thousand names. Clarabel has no useful warm start, so its re-solve is its cold solve: 69 ms to 2 s.

D2Tolerance Is in Weights

Run in OPT

OSQP stops when its residuals are below eps_abs + eps_rel·(size). Its default, 10⁻³, is a sensible number for a problem whose variables are around one. A weight in a 2,000-name book is around 5×10⁻⁴. Measured against Clarabel's solution at 2,000 names: at 10⁻³ the largest weight error was 1.4 times the average weight; at 10⁻⁴, 0.66; at 10⁻⁵, 0.08. Only the last is a portfolio you would send.

Speed and accuracy have to be read together. A rescaled version of the problem (weights in units of 1/n) with ρ = 30 stopped after 225 iterations — and its weights were off by 1.9 average weights. It had stopped early, not converged faster.

Check against a second solver once, on a problem of the size you run, and keep the tolerance that gets the weights within a tenth of an average weight. Then .po.parts on every solve is the cheap check that nothing has drifted.

With exec-tca-stack running, opt.q subscribes to execs on the chained tickerplant (5013), exactly as the TCA CEP does, so a slow solve can only ever back up the chain. Twelve Ways to Get This Wrong

Each of these produces a portfolio that solves, passes a status check, and is wrong. Most of them are in the labs as warnings; collected here they make a checklist.

01

Charging costs on the holding

Cost is a function of the trade, h − h0. Written on h, it penalises the book for existing and the optimiser sells good positions to save costs it would never pay.

02

Passing the whole of P

OSQP reads the upper triangle and mirrors it. Give it both halves and every off-diagonal term counts twice: the factor covariance doubles and nothing complains.

03

Forming V

X F Xᵀ is n² dense: 4 million nonzeros at 2,000 names, 100 million at 10,000. The factor rows keep it at about 60n. The answer is the same; the memory and the time are not.

04

Trusting "solved"

It means the residuals passed a tolerance. At OSQP's default tolerance, weights in a 2,000-name book were off by more than an average weight. Check the invariants (A2) and the tolerance (D2).

05

Leaving ρ at its default

On this problem it costs a factor of seven to ten from a cold start. The adaptive rule did not find the right value on its own. Set it, and refit the rule when the formulation or the units change.

06

Calling setup on every fill

It throws away the factorisation and the warm start, which are the two things that make the next solve cheap. A fill is .qp.update; setup is for a new universe.

07

Not spending the turnover

Move h0 on a fill but leave τ alone, and every re-solve may trade the whole budget again. Fills spend the budget; the problem has to know.

08

An equality budget, live

Fills leave cash. With the turnover spent, 1ᵀh = 1 may be unreachable and the problem infeasible. Allow a band, and reset the turnover window when it runs out.

09

Changing the sparsity and calling updmat

A new name, a new constraint, a factor that becomes exactly zero: each changes the pattern, and the values no longer line up. The shim refuses a wrong length; a right length in the wrong order it cannot catch.

10

Executing the whole MPO plan

Only the first period is a decision. The rest is there to stop the first from over-trading, and it is wrong by the time you get there.

11

Optimising each book, then netting

Each book pays, in its own objective, for trades that would have crossed. On the run above, that left 21 to 34 bps of firm utility on the table.

12

Benchmarking the solver on its own settings

The same problem took 21 seconds or 2 with one parameter changed, and 225 iterations with a looser stop that gave the wrong book. Compare solvers at the same accuracy, measured on the weights.

Production Notes

What changes when this stops being a lab. Four seams, each a short note.

A COLD SOLVER AT THE OPEN

The first solve of the day, and the solve after a universe change, are cold. Clarabel does those 1.6 to 4.7 times faster from 2,000 names up. A second shim with the same (P;q;A;l;u) inputs, and a .qp.warm from its answer, gives OSQP the best possible start for the day's loop.

MANY DESKS

A slot is about 5.6 MB per thousand names, and solves on different handles are independent. One optimiser process per desk or strategy behind the same chain, each with its own handles, scales across cores and machines the way any CEP does. One solve does not spread across cores: OSQP's builtin linear algebra is single-threaded.

REAL INPUTS

Volatility and ADV for the cost terms are a select … by sym over the HDB; α arrives from the research stack; X, F and Δ from the risk vendor once a day. Each lands as one update: q, the bounds, or the values of P and A. Only a new universe needs a new setup.

When the Warm Solve Stalls

Most re-solves after a fill take tens of iterations. A few take hundreds: at 5,000 names the 90th percentile was 300 ms against a 24 ms median. Those are the fills that change which constraints bind — a name hitting its box, the turnover window running out. Three rules keep the loop predictable. Cap the iterations of a warm solve (.qp.set[hs;enlist[`max_iter]!enlist 500]) and treat "maximum iterations reached" as a signal, not an answer. When it happens, solve cold — Clarabel, or a fresh OSQP setup — and .qp.warm the live handle from the result. And when the status is primal infeasible, change a bound rather than retrying: in opt.q that means resetting the turnover window, which is the bound live trading exhausts first.

Other Backends, Other Shapes

OSQP 1.x can be built against Intel MKL's Pardiso, which factorises on several threads, or CUDA, which runs the whole ADMM on a GPU. The release library setup.sh downloads uses OSQP's builtin, single-threaded algebra; the others need --from-source with an extra CMake option, and MKL and CUDA are not native to Apple silicon. Neither changes the shim, and neither was measured here. The same applies to code generation: OSQP can emit a fixed-size C solver for one problem shape, which is worth it only when the universe never changes.

Five Rules

If you remember nothing else, remember these. Each is the general form of several of the mistakes above.

1

Write it in the solver's shape

One shape, written by hand: split the absolute value, carry the factors as variables, keep P to its upper triangle. The rewriting is the modelling, and it is where the speed comes from.

2

Set up once, update often

A fill, an alpha, a limit, a risk aversion: each is an update to a problem that already exists. Only a new universe is a new problem.

3

Accuracy is in weights

Choose the tolerance and ρ by the error in the book, against a second solver, at the size you run. "Solved" is a statement about residuals, not about the portfolio.

4

Cold and warm are different jobs

An interior-point solver for the first solve; ADMM with a warm start for the loop; a cold solve again when the loop stalls. Benchmark each job, not the solver.

5

Optimise what you trade

Costs on the net trade, risk on the net book, the turnover the fills already spent. An optimiser that sees a different book from the one on the tickerplant is solving someone else's problem.

The first three are about the solver and the last two about the desk, but they share one idea: the problem you hand the solver should be the problem you have, in its shape, its units and its state — and the state changes on every fill.
